### Reconstructing a historical argument

10 practice marks

Reconstruct the lecture’s version of Shapin’s argument. Identify its data, claim, warrant, backing, qualifier, and reservation. Explain why the data do not establish the claim without a warrant.

Answer requirements:

- Use the lecture’s qualified reconstruction, not a claim that no scientific changes occurred.
- Do not invent Shapin’s omitted historiographic theory.

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

Ask what the diversity of practices is evidence for.

Separate a rule connecting evidence to conclusion from the support for accepting that rule.

</details>

<details><summary>Worked solution and rubric</summary>

The data are the variety of actors, viewpoints, and practices within the period. The claim is that these should not be represented as one singular, unified Scientific Revolution. The warrant is that developments lacking the relevant unity should not count as one unified event. Its backing would be a defensible account of historical explanation and what makes events unified; the lecture says this is not developed in the assigned excerpt. The qualifier is that this is the best available story, not an unrevisable proof. The reservation limits the discussion to the relevant European setting, framed in class as the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Diversity needs the warrant because a single historical development can potentially contain internal differences. The argument must explain why these particular differences defeat the proposed unity rather than merely occur within it.

- 2 marks: accurately identify both data and claim, 1 each.
- 2 marks: state the warrant and explain its role connecting diversity to lack of unity, 1 each.
- 2 marks: identify the needed backing and acknowledge that its detailed theory is not supplied, 1 each.
- 2 marks: state the qualifier and time/place reservation, 1 each.
- 2 marks: explain why internal diversity alone does not deductively exclude every unified historical account.

</details>

### Four causes and natural motion

10 practice marks

Explain Aristotle’s four causes using the clay pot. Then explain how rising fire and falling rocks illustrate teleology, and why Hobbes thinks this particular explanation risks circularity.

Answer requirements:

- Use cause in the lecture’s broad explanatory sense.
- Distinguish the historical view from an endorsement of it.

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

The material is not the same as the process that shaped it.

Ask whether the supposed purpose is known independently of the motion it explains.

</details>

<details><summary>Worked solution and rubric</summary>

The pot’s material cause is its clay; the clay’s properties help explain its properties. Its efficient cause is the process of shaping and firing it. Its formal cause is its shape or organizing design. Its final cause is its storage purpose. Teleology explains by purposes or goals. In the Aristotelian picture, fire rises and rocks fall because each seeks the place appropriate to its essence. Hobbes objects that if we infer the downward-seeking nature only from the fact that rocks fall, then explaining their fall by that nature merely repeats the observation in different words. No independently supported mechanism or explanatory basis has been added. This challenges that explanation’s informative value, not every possible purpose-based explanation.

- 4 marks: correctly explain and apply the four causes, 1 each.
- 2 marks: define teleology and apply it to natural motion, 1 each.
- 3 marks: explain the circular dependence between observed motion and attributed purpose.
- 1 mark: avoid treating the objection as proof that all purpose-language is meaningless.

</details>

### Observation and necessary connection

8 practice marks

Using al-Ghazali’s cotton example and the lecturer’s water-bottle example, distinguish observing a sequence from observing a necessary connection. State what the objection does and does not establish.

Answer requirements:

- Do not conclude that the examples prove there are no causes.
- Keep the claim about sensory access distinct from a prediction of what will happen next.

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

What is actually visible in each example?

Necessary means that the effect must follow, not merely that it did follow.

</details>

<details><summary>Worked solution and rubric</summary>

In the cotton case, we see flame applied and cotton burning. In the bottle case, we see the hand move and then the bottle move. In neither case is the claim that the second event had to occur an additional visible feature. A necessary connection would mean the effect could not fail under the relevant conditions; a sequence reports only what happened. The objection challenges the idea that sense experience directly reveals an essence guaranteeing the effect. It leaves room for further inference about causes. It neither proves that causes do not exist nor establishes that cotton exposed to flame will fail to burn.

- 2 marks: accurately describe what is observed in both examples, 1 each.
- 2 marks: explain the contrast between an actual sequence and necessity.
- 2 marks: connect the contrast to the criticism of directly sensing essences.
- 2 marks: give two appropriate limits on the conclusion, 1 each.

</details>

### Middle axioms and observational method

10 practice marks

What is a middle axiom? Use the mine’s copper example to contrast it with an axiom of the highest generality, list the four observational steps of Bacon’s method, and identify one reason the method cannot operate entirely without background understanding.

Answer requirements:

- Do not describe Bacon as rejecting all generalization.
- Do not treat one successful observation as proving the generalization.

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

A middle axiom lies between a single case and a sweeping universal principle.

Consider how an observer decides that a phenomenon is absent.

</details>

<details><summary>Worked solution and rubric</summary>

A middle axiom is an intermediate generalization drawn from particular experiences. The claim that all copper from one mine is ductile goes beyond a single sample but is narrower than a claim about all copper or the essential nature of an elemental kind. Bacon’s method accumulates observations, classifies cases where the phenomenon is present or absent, ranks degrees of its presence, and searches the recorded circumstances for regularities associated with it. These steps already require interpretation. For example, a cloudy morning must be distinguished from a morning on which the Sun actually failed to rise; making that distinction draws on knowledge about clouds, visibility, and the Sun. Bacon recommends gradual generalization, but the strict idea of beginning without any theory creates difficulties even for classifying the initial evidence.

- 2 marks: define a middle axiom and locate its level of generality.
- 2 marks: accurately explain the copper example and contrast its scope with a highest-generality claim.
- 4 marks: identify the four steps, 1 each.
- 2 marks: explain one concrete dependence on background understanding.

</details>

## Review quiz

### Original self-test: theory and observation

12 practice marks

Original self-test, not a verified exam-format sample. A student says: “To prove the Sun rises every day, record the light each morning. A cloudy morning still counts because the Sun always rises behind clouds. Enough such records remove all need for theory.” Identify the proposed evidence and conclusion, evaluate the reasoning, and distinguish the lecture’s objection from Hume’s problem of induction.

Answer requirements:

- Discuss the strict theory-free rule used in the classroom exercise.
- Include a limitation on the scope of the every-day claim.

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

Is the reason for counting a cloudy case independent of the conclusion?

Would a large number of records tell you how to interpret light without any background knowledge?

</details>

<details><summary>Worked solution and rubric</summary>

The evidence is a record of morning light, including cloudy mornings classified as sunrises; the conclusion is that the Sun rises every day without needing theoretical assumptions. The classification is not independent if its justification is simply that the Sun always rises: that already assumes the conclusion. Light is also a proxy for the event, and using it requires an account connecting daylight with the Sun rather than, for example, glowing clouds. More records do not remove that interpretive dependence. The unrestricted claim also needs geographical and temporal qualification because polar conditions do not display the same daily rising-and-setting cycle as the familiar local case. The lecture’s objection concerns how to identify relevant observations and classify cases without prior understanding. Hume’s problem instead asks what justifies extending observed regularities to unobserved cases or the future. Even temporarily granting that well-chosen repeated cases increase confidence leaves the classification problem intact.

- 2 marks: identify evidence and conclusion, 1 each.
- 3 marks: explain the circular use of the sunrise generalization to classify cloudy cases.
- 2 marks: explain why daylight is not a theory-free substitute for directly observing sunrise.
- 2 marks: explain the polar/location qualification.
- 3 marks: accurately distinguish the classification problem from Hume’s problem of induction.

</details>

### Original self-test: observation and ancient authority

10 practice marks

Original self-test, not a verified exam-format sample. Evaluate the claim: “Vesalius’s anatomical corrections prove that scientific innovators abandoned ancient authority.” Reconstruct the reasoning behind his restoration of Galen and explain the role of social status in the contrasting dissection story.

Answer requirements:

- Distinguish an investigator’s interpretation of his work from what a mismatch in anatomy independently proves.
- Do not claim that every discrepancy was demonstrably a copying error.

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

Which could be blamed: Galen’s original work or the received version?

Who had access to the body, and who had authority to speak?

</details>

<details><summary>Worked solution and rubric</summary>

The claim is too strong. Vesalius’s corrections show the importance of examining human anatomy, but the lecture presents him as restoring Galen’s knowledge rather than abandoning Galen’s authority. His reasoning can be reconstructed as follows: Galen’s original knowledge was trusted; the received text could have been corrupted by repeated copying; observations disagreed with that text; therefore correcting the text could restore what Galen originally knew. A mismatch alone does not prove corruption rather than Galen’s error, so the reconstruction explains Vesalius’s commitment, not the independent truth of every premise. In the contrasting dissection story, a high-status reader controlled the authoritative description, while a lower-status barber-surgeon handled the body without equivalent authority to challenge the text. Thus access to evidence and the social power to have it accepted could diverge. The case shows that observation and respect for antiquity could coexist.

- 2 marks: reject the overgeneralized conclusion while retaining the value of anatomical observation.
- 3 marks: reconstruct the restoration reasoning, including trust in Galen and possible textual corruption.
- 2 marks: explain why anatomical disagreement does not identify its historical source by itself.
- 2 marks: explain the reader/dissector status difference and its effect on evidence.
- 1 mark: state the resulting complication for a simple authority-to-observation narrative.

</details>

### Original self-test: testing an instrument

12 practice marks

Original self-test, not a verified exam-format sample. Why did checking Galileo’s telescope on a building inscription not automatically settle whether its observations of Jupiter were reliable for an Aristotelian audience? Explain the relevance of observer disagreement, the Moon example, and the limits of what Jupiter’s moons would establish.

Answer requirements:

- Evaluate the historical audience’s available reasons rather than assuming modern knowledge.
- Do not equate criticism of the instrument with proof that Galileo was mistaken.

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

What difference did the audience assume between Earth and sky?

Does one non-Earth-centered orbit by itself specify the whole solar system?

</details>

<details><summary>Worked solution and rubric</summary>

Reading an inscription and independently checking it tests the telescope on terrestrial objects. The audience’s Aristotelian cosmology treated the heavens as fundamentally different, made of quintessence and governed by different patterns. Trusting terrestrial performance in the celestial domain therefore required a further justification, which the lecture says available optical and cosmological theory did not adequately supply. Difficult early lenses and observers’ inability to agree on seeing the moons also weakened the immediate public demonstration, without proving that no moons existed. The Moon was not a neutral escape from the problem: alleged craters challenged heavenly perfection, while the Moon still belonged to the disputed celestial domain. If Jupiter’s moons really orbit Jupiter, they refute the claim that every celestial orbit is centered on Earth in the presented model. They do not alone prove all the claims of a Sun-centered cosmology. The case illustrates the need to assess observations together with instrument reliability and background assumptions.

- 2 marks: explain what the inscription test establishes and its domain limit.
- 3 marks: explain the terrestrial/celestial distinction and the need for further reliability support.
- 2 marks: explain observer disagreement without turning it into a conclusive refutation.
- 2 marks: explain why the Moon example encounters the same objection.
- 2 marks: state the limited challenge posed by Jupiter’s moons rather than claiming proof of an entire cosmology.
- 1 mark: draw the general lesson about observation and background understanding.

</details>

### Original self-test: Boyle, Hobbes, and Newton

15 practice marks

Original self-test, not a verified exam-format sample. Compare Boyle, Hobbes, and Newton on how science should connect evidence and explanation. Include Boyle’s reporting practices, Hobbes’s use of alternative causes, and Newton’s crucial experiment. Explain how the comparison supports Shapin without proving his conclusion eternally true.

Answer requirements:

- Do not characterize Boyle as uninterested in causes.
- Distinguish Newton’s claimed certainty from an independently established guarantee.

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

Compare the direction of inference, reporting detail, and strength of conclusion.

Shapin’s conclusion also depends on what counts as a unified historical event.

</details>

<details><summary>Worked solution and rubric</summary>

Boyle began with carefully described effects and cautiously investigated causes. He recorded apparatus, procedures, circumstances, and witnesses to support checking, and separated observations from theoretical speculation. Hobbes supplied rival explanations, challenging the move from an observed effect to one supposedly established mechanism. He argued for proceeding from rationally established causes to knowledge of effects. Newton treated the two-prism experiment as a decisive discriminator: isolated red light remained red after further refraction rather than producing another rainbow. He emphasized experimental weight rather than many repetitions and claimed theoretical certainty, without Boyle’s level of contextual reporting. These are substantive differences in method, evidential emphasis, and confidence among major figures of the same period. They support Shapin’s objection to a single shared observation-first method. They do not establish every possible version of his historical claim deductively, because diversity may coexist with some kinds of unity and the historical account remains limited by evidence and interpretive standards.

- 4 marks: explain Boyle’s effects-to-causes direction, caution, reporting detail, and separation of observations/theory, 1 each.
- 3 marks: explain Hobbes’s alternatives, their inferential effect, and his causes-to-effects ideal, 1 each.
- 4 marks: explain Newton’s experiment, observed result, emphasis on weight, and claim of certainty, 1 each.
- 2 marks: show how these methodological differences support Shapin’s challenge.
- 2 marks: explain why that support remains qualified rather than an eternal proof.

</details>

### Validity and the hidden standard of historical unity

12 practice marks

Consider this reconstruction: Premise 1: Boyle, Hobbes, and Newton disagreed about scientific method. Premise 2: If participants disagree, their activities cannot belong to one historical event. Conclusion: Their activities cannot belong to one Scientific Revolution. Is the argument deductively valid? Is its warrant well supported by the lecture? Construct a possible counterexample to Premise 2, then revise the conclusion to one better supported by the lecture.

Answer requirements:

- Your counterexample may be hypothetical; label it as such.
- Do not confuse a valid form with true or justified premises.
- Keep the revised conclusion within the lecture’s evidence.

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

If both premises were true, could the conclusion be false?

Imagine a bounded event deliberately organized to contain disagreement.

</details>

<details><summary>Worked solution and rubric</summary>

The argument is deductively valid: if the participants disagree and any disagreement rules out membership in a single historical event, the conclusion follows. Validity does not establish the second premise. The lecture says a theory of historical unity is needed; it does not show that all participant disagreement excludes a unified event. A hypothetical counterexample is one scheduled scientific meeting at which participants debate rival methods. It remains one identifiable meeting despite disagreement, so disagreement alone does not rule out every kind of event unity. This challenges Premise 2 rather than the argument’s form. A better-supported conclusion is that Boyle, Hobbes, and Newton cannot accurately be portrayed as uniformly adopting one theory-free, observation-first method. Their differences support a more complex history while leaving open whether another defensible standard could group their work within a broader Scientific Revolution.

- 2 marks: correctly identify deductive validity and explain the conditional form.
- 2 marks: distinguish validity from support for the premises.
- 2 marks: identify the unsupported strength of the disagreement-to-disunity warrant.
- 3 marks: supply a clearly hypothetical counterexample and explain why it challenges Premise 2.
- 3 marks: formulate a narrower conclusion supported by the methodological evidence and preserve the remaining historical question.

</details>

### Evaluating an inference to the best explanation

12 practice marks

A fictional investigator reproduces Boyle’s falling-barometer result many times and argues: “My proposed mechanism would produce this result. The result occurs. Therefore my mechanism is certainly correct.” Reconstruct the inference, explain the problem, and specify what further argumentative work would be needed to defend the mechanism without pretending the supplied lecture gives Hobbes’s exact alternative physics.

Answer requirements:

- Use only generic rival mechanisms, not invented historical claims about Hobbes.
- Explain why repetition helps in one respect but does not automatically settle the cause.

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

Could a different mechanism predict the same result?

An inference to the best explanation needs a comparison, not just a fit.

</details>

<details><summary>Worked solution and rubric</summary>

The argument says: if mechanism M operates under the setup, result R follows; R occurs; therefore M is certainly the cause. As a deduction, this does not guarantee its conclusion because another mechanism N might also produce R. Repeating R improves support for the result’s regularity under the procedure, but does not by itself distinguish M from N if both predict the repeated pattern. As an inference to the best explanation, the argument would need to identify relevant alternatives, explain why M accounts for the evidence better, and justify the standards used to compare them. Further experiments could be designed so that the alternatives predict different outcomes, with attention to instrument reliability and experimental conditions. Hobbes’s role in the lecture is to press the availability of alternative causal stories; their precise physical details are not supplied. Their existence blocks the claimed certainty, but does not alone establish that M is false or that N is preferable.

- 2 marks: reconstruct the conditional premise, observation, and causal conclusion.
- 3 marks: explain a possible same-result alternative and the failure of deductive guarantee.
- 2 marks: explain what repetition supports and what it leaves unresolved.
- 3 marks: propose comparative explanatory assessment or discriminating evidence with justified conditions.
- 2 marks: preserve both limits: no invented Hobbes mechanism and no automatic refutation of M.

</details>

### Reconstructing and limiting a crucial experiment

14 practice marks

For this simplified self-test only, suppose theory A predicts that an isolated red beam will become a new rainbow in a second prism, while theory B predicts it will remain red. The beam remains red. Reconstruct a decisive argument for B, make its required assumptions explicit, and explain why repeating the test many times is neither automatically necessary nor automatically sufficient for certainty. Relate your answer to Newton and Boyle.

Answer requirements:

- Treat A and B as simplified exercise alternatives, not complete historical theories.
- Do not use the disputed middle row of the prism diagram.
- Distinguish support for B from proof that no other account is possible.

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

Eliminating A establishes B only if the alternatives really are exhaustive.

Repeated trials can check reliability without eliminating an unconsidered theory.

</details>

<details><summary>Worked solution and rubric</summary>

A decisive version needs these premises: A or B is the correct account; under the specified conditions A must produce a rainbow and B must leave the beam red; the conditions were met; and the outcome was reliably observed to remain red. The result excludes A under those assumptions, and the exhaustive A-or-B premise then permits the conclusion B. If other theories are possible, excluding A does not alone establish B as the uniquely true account. If the beam was not properly isolated or the apparatus was unreliable, the link from theory to observed outcome also weakens. Newton’s crucial-experiment ideal emphasizes a setup whose outcomes discriminate between rivals, so sheer repetition adds little to the logical contrast once the conditions are secure. Boyle’s emphasis on detailed reporting and checking highlights why confidence in those conditions cannot simply be assumed. Repetition may detect errors or variability, but repeatedly obtaining the same result does not prove that A and B exhaust all explanations. Thus evidential weight and repetition answer different questions.

- 3 marks: reconstruct the competing predictions and exclusion of A from the reliable result.
- 3 marks: state and explain the exhaustive-alternatives assumption needed to conclude B decisively.
- 2 marks: identify proper setup and reliable observation as conditions of the inference.
- 2 marks: explain Newton’s emphasis on discriminating weight rather than sheer number.
- 2 marks: connect Boyle’s reporting/checking to confidence in experimental conditions.
- 2 marks: explain both the usefulness and the limits of repetition.

</details>

### Can an empiricist scientific community avoid testimony?

14 practice marks

A fictional scientific society adopts this policy: “Accept only what you have personally witnessed. A detailed report with named witnesses eliminates all reliance on testimony.” Use Boyle’s practices and the lecture’s distinction between general experience and specific experiences to evaluate the policy. Propose a more defensible policy within the taught scope, explaining why it is neither blind deference nor completely theory-free knowledge.

Answer requirements:

- Do not invent a course policy or claim the fictional policy was actually adopted verbatim.
- Include why the date and location of an air-pressure experiment can matter.

<details><summary>Hints</summary>

Reading that someone witnessed an event is not the same as witnessing it yourself.

Replication offers a route to checking, but not everyone can repeat everything.

</details>

<details><summary>Worked solution and rubric</summary>

The two parts of the policy conflict. A person who accepts a report because it names witnesses is still relying on others’ testimony, not on personal witnessing of the reported event. Boyle’s public demonstrations reduced that dependence for those present, but everyone could not attend every experiment. His detailed reports offered another route to checking: describe the apparatus and procedure, identify circumstances and witnesses, and make replication possible. This emphasis concerns specific experiences—events at particular times and places—rather than only the generic claim that a phenomenon usually occurs. For air pressure, weather and altitude can change results, so the date and location may help explain differences between trials. A more defensible policy would favor direct checking where feasible, require transparent procedures and contextual reporting, permit cautious reliance on others’ reports, and separate observation claims from causal interpretations. This is not blind deference because it makes challenges and replication possible. It is not theory-free because deciding what the apparatus measures, which circumstances matter, and how data support a causal claim requires background understanding and warrants.

- 3 marks: explain why reports and witness names do not eliminate testimony and identify the policy’s conflict.
- 2 marks: explain the practical limits of universal personal witnessing.
- 2 marks: distinguish specific experiences from generalized experience.
- 2 marks: explain the relevance of weather/date and altitude/location, 1 each.
- 3 marks: propose a defensible combination of checking, detailed reporting, cautious testimony, and separation of observation from explanation.
- 2 marks: explain why the proposal avoids blind deference but still requires theory and inferential warrants.

</details>
