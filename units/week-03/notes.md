## History makes arguments: Shapin’s challenge to a simple revolution story

## Historians explain and interpret
History is not merely a list of things that happened. Historians select and connect evidence to argue for an interpretation of events. Their arguments may extend across many pages rather than appear within a single paragraph. They can argue that a familiar story is more complicated than it seems, that one development was a major cause of another, or that a particular framework helps us understand events. These are arguments because the evidence is meant to support a conclusion, not merely supply background facts.

The lecture identifies the reading as Steven Shapin’s *The Scientific Revolution*. Shapin challenges the idea that there was one singular, discrete event—one clearly bounded, unified transformation—that deserves the name **the Scientific Revolution**. This does not mean that nothing changed or that the people and discoveries discussed were imaginary. It challenges how those changes are grouped and interpreted.

## The simplified story being challenged
The target narrative runs approximately as follows: Europeans once accepted ancient books on authority; then, during the Scientific Revolution, they rejected those books, began observing and experimenting, and modern science emerged. Shapin’s objection is not that observation and experiment were unimportant. It is that this contrast makes both the earlier period and the later practices too uniform. Aristotle already valued experience, some innovators sought to restore ancient knowledge, and early modern investigators disagreed about what experiments could establish.

The lecture also warns against projecting modern mass communication backward. Scientific discussions involved a small, unusually situated part of European society, often people with enough wealth and leisure to investigate nature. Most people did not participate in these debates. Even within that small scientific community, practices and views differed. A change among particular investigators should not automatically be described as a simultaneous transformation of an entire population.

**Natural philosophy** was the earlier name for inquiry into nature, roughly corresponding to what we now call science. Before about the nineteenth century, this inquiry was understood as a branch of philosophy. When the reading discusses natural philosophers, it is discussing investigators of the natural world, not a separate activity unrelated to science.

## Reconstructing and qualifying Shapin’s argument

An **argument reconstruction** makes explicit how evidence is supposed to support a conclusion, including connections that an author leaves unstated. The lecture applies the following argument-analysis vocabulary to Shapin:

- **Data:** the evidence offered. Here, many different actors held different views and used different practices during the period commonly called the Scientific Revolution.
- **Claim:** the conclusion the evidence is meant to support. Here, there was no single unified event adequately captured by the simple revolution narrative.
- **Warrant:** the rule connecting data to claim. The proposed warrant is that a development lacking the relevant unity should not count as one singular, unified event.
- **Backing:** support for accepting that warrant. In this case, some account of how historical explanations work and what makes events sufficiently unified is needed. The assigned excerpt, as described in the lecture, omits Shapin’s historiographic notes. **Historiography** is reflection on how history should be written and explained. Consequently, the lecture identifies a needed kind of backing rather than supplying Shapin’s complete theory.
- **Qualifier:** how strongly the claim is asserted. The reconstruction presents this as the best available historical account, not a conclusion guaranteed never to change.
- **Reservation:** a limit on the claim’s application. The lecture frames this discussion around Europe in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Seventeenth century means the 1600s; eighteenth century means the 1700s. Some examples used to explain the background predate that framing.

The reasoning link matters: differences among investigators count against a story that describes everyone as following one new method. However, the mere presence of differences does not logically rule out every possible way of grouping events together. A defender of a unified revolution could allow internal disagreement. To reach the broader conclusion, Shapin needs a defensible standard of unity and evidence that the differences matter under that standard. The lecture explicitly leaves this background issue open rather than proving the warrant independently.

Historical conclusions are also constrained by the surviving evidence. Historians often work with documents that happened to survive and remain accessible. Access is uneven across regions, including where colonial destruction or missing records limits what can be known. New documents or new interpretive methods may therefore change the best explanation. The qualifier is not an admission that all stories are equally good: it says the preferred account is supported by the evidence and methods currently available, while remaining revisable.

The final comparison of Boyle, Hobbes, and Newton supplies further evidence for this reconstruction. Their disagreements support a more complicated account; they do not establish Shapin’s thesis with mathematical certainty or for all time.

## Aristotle: an integrated cosmos, natural motion, and four causes

## Why this worldview was influential
The lecture presents Aristotle’s influence as partly a result of the coherence of his worldview: his accounts of matter, motion, the heavens, and knowledge fitted together. His writings were influential for many centuries in European and Islamic intellectual traditions. The lecture notes that some works lost to parts of Europe were preserved in the Eastern Roman Empire and the Islamic world; this is not a story of all ancient learning simply disappearing everywhere.

A **cosmology** is an account of the structure and workings of the universe. In the Aristotelian cosmology presented here, the Earth is round and stationary at the center. The Sun, Moon, planets, and stars circle the Earth. Do not confuse this Earth-centered account with a flat-Earth view.

The system distinguishes five elements: earth, water, air, fire, and **quintessence**, the special substance of the heavens. Terrestrial objects—things down here on Earth—are mixtures of the first four. Each has an **essence**, an underlying nature that defines what it is and how it characteristically behaves.

## Natural motion and proper places
Earth tends toward the center; water settles above earth but below air; air occupies the region above water and below fire; fire rises above air. These motions are understood in terms of things reaching the places appropriate to their natures. A thrown rock returns downward, while a flame rises. In this picture, those familiar observations disclose what these kinds of things naturally seek to do.

The heavenly realm is different. Quintessence is associated with perfect circular motion and enduring regularity rather than decay, friction, or breakdown. Long-observed celestial cycles made this distinction appear plausible: the heavens seemed to repeat reliably, whereas earthly objects wore out and changed. Thus **terrestrial physics**, concerning earthly things, and **celestial physics**, concerning the heavens, were treated as importantly different. This distinction later matters to the debate over whether a telescope tested on Earth should also be trusted in the sky.

## Four kinds of cause
For Aristotle, a complete explanation could involve four kinds of **cause**. Here, “cause” is broader than a preceding physical event: it means a way of explaining why a thing is as it is.

| Cause | Meaning | Clay-pot example |
|---|---|---|
| Material | What something is made of | The pot is clay, so the properties of clay help explain its properties. |
| Efficient | The processes or actions that bring it about | A potter shapes the clay using hands and a wheel, then fires it in a kiln. |
| Formal | The form, structure, or organizing pattern | The pot has a particular shape or design; the lecture compares this with a blueprint. |
| Final | The purpose or goal | The pot was made to store something. |

The chairs at the front of the classroom illustrate why formal and efficient causes are not identical. Different acts of manufacture can produce chairs sharing the same form. Their common structure is one explanatory feature; the separate processes that made them are another.

Explaining by goals or purposes is called **teleology**. Applied to natural motion, it treats a rock’s movement toward its proper place as goal-directed. The lecture next asks whether that explanation tells us more than the observation that rocks fall.

## Knowing essences, seeing necessity, and replacing teleology with mechanism

## Aristotle’s epistemology
**Epistemology** concerns knowledge: how we acquire it and what justifies it. In the lecture’s simplified account, Aristotle thought that a suitable observer, under conditions allowing reliable perception, could sense a thing’s form or essence. Seeing fire rise could reveal rising as part of fire’s nature, not merely report one event.

The conditions matter. Intoxication, fog, or impaired eyesight could interfere with perception. The lecturer also criticizes the exclusionary assumptions about who counted as a suitable rational observer, including Aristotle’s privileging of Greek men. These are historical assumptions being criticized, not standards endorsed by the course.

If an essence includes a causal connection, the connection is treated as **necessary**: given the relevant nature and circumstances, the effect must occur. **Deduction** is inference intended to guarantee its conclusion if its premises are true. If burning upon contact with flame belongs necessarily to cotton’s nature, one could use that premise to deduce that cotton exposed to flame will burn. But the supposed guarantee depends on actually knowing the premise about its essence.

## Al-Ghazali: observation does not display necessity
The lecture introduces the eleventh-century philosopher al-Ghazali in the context of debates about Aristotle and Islam. His objection targets the claim that perception directly reveals necessary causal connections.

Consider bringing flame to cotton. We see flame applied and cotton burning. Do we also see that burning *had to* follow? The experienced sequence—one event followed by another—does not itself display necessity. The water-bottle example makes the same point: seeing a hand move and then the bottle move is not the same as seeing that the second event could not have failed to occur.

The distinction is between **observing succession** and **knowing a necessary connection**. We may infer an account of causes from experience, but that is an additional intellectual step, not something simply visible alongside the flame or bottle. Imagining cotton not burning helps expose the difference between seeing what occurred and perceiving that no alternative was possible. It does not, by itself, establish that cotton will actually fail to burn or that there are no causes.

The lecture notes that a related critique is often associated with Hume, but al-Ghazali made this objection centuries earlier. The point here is the limit of what sense experience directly provides, not a full presentation of either philosopher’s theory.

## Hobbes: a purpose can merely rename the effect
Hobbes criticizes explaining heaviness as an endeavor to reach the center of the Earth. If the only reason for attributing that endeavor is that the object falls, then saying it falls because it endeavors to fall adds no independent explanation. The reasoning circles back to the observation it was supposed to explain: rocks fall because their nature seeks downward motion, and we identify that nature because they fall.

This criticism concerns the explanatory value of that account. It does not mean that every use of purpose-language is automatically empty.

## Mechanical philosophy
**Mechanism**, or the **mechanical philosophy**, explains phenomena through physical processes such as motion, shape, contact, and the pushing and pulling of bodies. In the lecture’s comparison, efficient causes become the acceptable explanatory model. Formal and final causes lose their status as independent explanations, while material considerations are folded into the mechanical account. Matter does not cease to matter: its arrangement and behavior are described mechanically.

The slide crosses out material, formal, and final causes, leaving efficient cause. This represents a prominent program in the period, not unanimous agreement. Aristotelians and Aristotelian ideas remained. That coexistence itself cautions against treating the period as a single clean intellectual replacement.

## Bacon’s reform: empiricism, middle axioms, and an observational method

## A new instrument for knowledge
Francis Bacon’s *Novum Organum* appeared in 1620 as a call to reform how knowledge was produced and justified. Its title, meaning “new instrument,” challenges Aristotle’s *Organon*, associated with logic and reasoning. Bacon was not simply an isolated outsider: the lecture describes him as a politically prominent figure whose proposals resonated with existing concerns. A famous book can express a broader movement rather than create it single-handedly.

The slogan “Aristotle favored deduction; Bacon favored induction” is explicitly a drastic simplification. **Induction** extends from observed cases toward a more general conclusion, rather than guaranteeing that conclusion solely through deductive form. Aristotle did not ignore experience, and Bacon’s criticism concerns especially the speed and confidence with which general principles were reached.

**Empiricism** locates the proper basis of knowledge in sense experience. Bacon’s reform rhetoric called for rebuilding knowledge on that foundation rather than trusting inherited authority. In the strongest version presented in the lecture, investigators should set aside tradition and testimony—what other people tell them—and encounter nature directly. This encouraged distrust of the social transmission of knowledge. The lecture questions whether that ideal could ever be followed literally.

## Do not jump straight to the highest generality
An **axiom**, in this discussion, is a principle used in reasoning. Bacon contrasts two routes:

1. Move rapidly from a few particular experiences to principles of the highest generality, then derive intermediate conclusions from them.
2. Move gradually from particular experiences through increasingly general principles, reaching the broadest principles last.

Seeing several fires rise does not by itself adequately support a sweeping account of the essence of fire, one of the five elemental kinds in the worldview. Calling it one of five elements concerns the breadth of the classification; it does not establish that exactly one-fifth of the universe’s quantity of matter is fire.

Bacon recommends **middle axioms**: generalizations broader than one observation but narrower than claims about universal essences. The lecture’s example is that all copper from a particular mine is [ductile](https://copper.org/resources/properties/703_5/) (able to deform without breaking). The relevant point is its limited scope: it does not yet claim something about all copper everywhere. The lecturer illustrates the material property by describing copper deforming under a hammer. The example teaches a step in generalization, not a complete account of material testing.

## The Baconian method as presented
After the rhetorical preliminary of setting aside inherited Aristotelian assumptions:

1. **Accumulate observations** of the phenomenon of interest, recording what happens and the surrounding circumstances.
2. **Classify cases by presence and absence:** distinguish cases in which the phenomenon occurs from cases in which it does not.
3. **Rank degrees of presence:** compare stronger, weaker, or intermediate cases rather than treating every occurrence as identical.
4. **Look for regularities:** identify circumstances that accompany the phenomenon, differ when it is absent, or vary with its degree, as clues to what is responsible for it.

Recording context is essential to the last step: an isolated list saying only that an event happened gives little basis for identifying which circumstances matter. The next exercise tests whether even these classifications can be made without bringing any prior understanding to the observations.

## Worked example: can pure observation establish that the Sun rises every day?

The class tests the Baconian method using the proposed generalization that the Sun rises every day. The exercise deliberately imposes an unusually strict rule: discard prior knowledge and work only from present sense experience. Its point is to expose what this rule prevents us from doing, not to recommend abandoning our ordinary knowledge of the Sun.

## Start with observations
An investigator could observe before sunrise, at noon, during the evening, at sunset, and at night, recording what was visible. On a clear morning, seeing the Sun emerge is straightforward. Clouds create a classification problem: if the Sun cannot be seen, is this a case in which it failed to rise, or a case in which its rise was hidden?

Ordinarily, we use background knowledge to choose the second interpretation. But under the exercise’s strict rule, that knowledge has been excluded. Using the very claim being tested—that the Sun rises every day—to classify every cloudy case as a sunrise would assume the conclusion instead of independently supporting it.

## Proposed solutions and their limits
- **Use daylight rather than a visible Sun.** This is sensible if we already know daylight comes from the Sun. Without that background connection, the observation of light alone does not distinguish sunlight behind clouds from an alternative such as clouds themselves glowing.
- **Use warmth.** This also requires a connection between the Sun and heat. Moreover, cloudy days may be warm or cold, so the absence of warmth is not a reliable test of whether the Sun rose.
- **Use the Moon’s appearance.** This draws on previously recognized regularities linking the visibility of the Sun and Moon. It therefore does not satisfy the requirement to use no prior understanding.
- **Suppose the Sun is always present but sometimes blocked.** This is a possible account, but introducing it is already theorizing about what causes the appearances. The question becomes how that account is supported.
- **Rank partly cloudy cases by visibility.** This resembles Bacon’s ranking step, but degrees of visibility do not automatically measure degrees of the Sun’s actual presence. Interpreting the ranking still requires an account of obstruction.
- **Fly above the clouds.** This can provide additional observations, but raises questions about locations, repeated observation, and how long to continue. It is not a simple replacement for all background knowledge.

Location also changes the phenomenon itself. The discussion invokes polar conditions and the lecturer’s summer experience in Iceland, where the Sun did not set. If it does not set during the relevant interval, there is no ordinary daily sunrise cycle there. Thus an unrestricted claim about every day everywhere is not the same as a claim about the familiar cycle at a particular location. An investigation must specify what it is generalizing about.

## What this establishes
A **theory**, in this discussion, includes an organized understanding of how things work, not necessarily a grand formal system. Such understanding helps us decide what to observe, which cases count as presence or absence, what conditions matter, and what would count as a counterexample—a case incompatible with a proposed generalization. Seeing no Sun through clouds is not automatically a counterexample to its rising; it may be a limitation on access to the event.

This is not the same as **Hume’s problem of induction**, the question of what justifies extending observed regularities to unobserved cases or the future. The lecturer temporarily sets that issue aside and grants that accumulating appropriate cases can increase confidence. A problem remains earlier in the process: without background understanding, we struggle to identify and classify the appropriate cases in the first place.

The result is not that observations are useless or that every theory is equally good. It is that the strongest ideal of entirely theory-free observation is not an adequate account of how inquiry gets started. Bacon’s call to attend more carefully to experience can be valuable without its clean-slate rhetoric being a literal description of actual scientific practice. This supports Shapin’s distinction between a founding story of science and the more complicated activities of investigators.

## Vesalius and Galen: observation can accompany respect for authority

Galen’s writings served as authoritative medical texts for centuries. The lecture explains that restrictions on human dissection led Galen to rely on animals, including monkeys, and that some resulting anatomical descriptions did not fit human bodies.

The simple heroic story contrasts a high-status reader of Galen with a lower-status barber-surgeon doing the manual work of dissection. The reader could announce what the book said should be present even when the person cutting the body encountered something different. The division of labor mattered because the person closest to the physical evidence did not necessarily possess the social authority to challenge the text publicly. This illustrates how observation and authority can be related through institutions and status, not merely through an individual’s willingness to look.

Vesalius’s human dissections revealed mismatches between bodies and received anatomical descriptions. In that respect, the story really does involve the value of examining nature rather than simply repeating a text. But Shapin’s complication concerns how Vesalius understood his own project: he saw himself as restoring Galen’s pure medical knowledge, not simply overthrowing Galen.

That interpretation depended on the transmission of ancient texts. Repeated handwritten copying could introduce omissions, transcription mistakes, and fragmentary versions. Scribes and copying practices varied, and correction procedures did not eliminate all errors. Someone strongly committed to Galen’s authority could therefore explain an apparent error in the received text as corruption during transmission rather than a mistake by Galen himself.

The reasoning is conditional: **if** the original Galen was right and **if** the surviving text disagreed with sound observation, then damage in transmission could explain the disagreement. Observing a mismatch does not establish those premises or prove that a scribe was responsible. It explains how Vesalius could combine new anatomical work with continued reverence for an ancient author.

This is a counterexample to the strongest claim that new observation necessarily meant rejection of ancient authority. It is not evidence that observation made no difference. The contrast between restoration and rejection is exactly why the actual history is more complicated than a simple victory of senses over books.

## Galileo’s telescope: evidence depends on an instrument’s credibility

## The observations and their significance
Galileo did not invent the telescope, but built an unusually capable early version. The lecture describes approximately 20-fold magnification and hand-ground lenses: impressive at the time, but difficult to use by modern standards.

He reported four moons orbiting Jupiter. In the Aristotelian picture presented in class, celestial bodies orbit Earth. If the reported moons really orbit Jupiter, they challenge the claim that Earth is the center of every celestial orbit. That observation alone does not establish every part of a Sun-centered system. Galileo was a **Copernican**, supporting the view that Earth goes around the Sun, but challenging universal Earth-centered orbits and proving a complete alternative cosmology are different achievements.

## Why “just look” was not enough
Two problems arose when others looked through the instrument:

1. **Not everyone saw the reported objects.** Early optics and the difficulty of using the telescope limited the shared experience Galileo could produce. The lecture recounts an occasion on which only one of twenty learned observers reported seeing the moons. This illustrates a problem of agreement among observers, not a controlled statistical experiment proving Galileo wrong.
2. **The instrument’s application to the heavens required justification.** One could point it across a plaza, read a building’s inscription, and independently check the result on Earth. But this established terrestrial performance. For an audience who believed the heavens were made of fundamentally different material, it did not automatically establish celestial performance.

An **instrument** extends observation, but its outputs become evidence only when there are reasons to trust what it does in the relevant circumstances. The people being persuaded could reasonably ask why a device tested on earthly things should reveal otherwise invisible features of quintessence. Publishing the result would communicate the claim, not by itself answer that reliability question.

The lecture presents the available optical theory—the theory of how light and lenses work—and cosmological understanding as insufficient to give Galileo the decisive theoretical assurance his critics wanted. Our confidence today should not be projected back onto an audience that lacked our understanding and accepted a different account of the sky.

## Why the Moon did not settle the issue
Galileo also reported craters or irregularities on the Moon. These conflicted with the expectation of perfect heavenly bodies. It may seem that the visible Moon offered a convenient intermediate test between terrestrial objects and distant moons of Jupiter. But in the relevant Aristotelian division, the Moon still belonged to the celestial realm. The claim of lunar irregularities therefore reopened both disputes: whether heavenly bodies were perfect and whether the telescope could reliably show otherwise.

The broader lesson is not that Galileo was wrong or that critics should never revise a worldview. It is that new instrument readings, established background beliefs, and theories of instrument operation must be evaluated together. The case complicates a picture in which trustworthy scientific knowledge simply appears when someone looks without a theory.

## Boyle: specific experiences, the vacuum pump, and the barometer

## Experience in general versus specific experiences
Shapin distinguishes two ways of using experience. **Experience in general** means familiar, commonsense regularities: fire rises, rocks fall, and people usually see certain things happen. The identity of an observer and the date of an observation are not part of the generalized statement. Aristotle’s appeal to experience therefore should not be confused with having no interest in the senses.

**Specific experiences** are particular observations made by identifiable people at a time and place, under stated conditions. Robert Boyle’s experimental work emphasizes this second approach. Recording particulars makes it possible to ask whether conditions affected a result and whether another investigator could reproduce it.

## The vacuum apparatus
Boyle’s pump removed air from a glass chamber, creating a relatively strong but not perfect **vacuum**, a space depleted of air. The lecture emphasizes that the nature of air and the explanation of air pressure were disputed: investigators did not share an established modern molecular account. Some considered air particulate—made of small pieces—while others considered it continuous. Even the increasing difficulty of turning the pump’s crank supplied information about what the apparatus was doing.

Creating the low-air space was itself scientifically interesting, but Boyle also placed other apparatus inside it to investigate what changed.

## Torricelli’s barometer and Boyle’s experiment
A **barometer** measures atmospheric pressure. The reference arrangement uses a glass tube filled with mercury, inverted with its opening under the surface of a mercury reservoir. Some mercury descends, leaving a space above the remaining column. Atmospheric pressure acts on the reservoir, and the height of the column supplies information about that pressure. The lecture’s diagram labels a 760 mm column, but this is a reference illustration, not a reported measurement from Boyle’s particular trial.

The barometer could first be observed in open air, where its level varied. Boyle then put it inside the vacuum chamber. As he pumped air out, the mercury column fell markedly. This created a concrete relation between an intervention—removing air—and an observed effect—a falling column.

## An observation is not yet a causal explanation
The reported sequence does not, by itself, settle the complete mechanical account of why the column falls. Science seeks more than an enormous list of observations: it seeks systematic explanations connecting them. Boyle nevertheless wanted to distinguish what he thought was securely observed from his less certain account of the cause.

His reports therefore separated observations from theoretical speculation. A reader could accept or investigate the described experimental events without automatically accepting Boyle’s proposed explanation. This is a cautious way of building upward from sense data, consistent with one strand of Bacon’s program. It does not mean Boyle believed science should permanently stop at reporting facts.

## The Royal Society: witnessing, replication, and the limits of rejecting testimony

The **Royal Society of London for Improving Natural Knowledge** institutionalized scientific cooperation under a royal charter. The lecture slide dates its founding to 1660, identifies Boyle as a founding member, and describes it as the oldest scientific society still in existence. It presents an initially small community, starting with twelve members and growing over time.

Its motto, **Nullius in verba**, is explained in the lecture as an instruction not to take someone’s word for a claim merely on authority. This expresses the Baconian emphasis on checking nature rather than accepting a statement because Galen, Aristotle, or another respected person wrote it.

## Making observations public
Boyle wanted other people to witness experiments directly. For example, he could bring the pump and enclosed barometer to a meeting, operate the pump, and let those present see the column fall. They would then have their own sense experience rather than depend entirely on his testimony.

However, it is impractical for everyone to attend every experiment. Scientific cooperation cannot literally eliminate all reliance on what other people say or write. As the community grows, bringing every potential reader into the same room becomes increasingly difficult.

## Reports as an imperfect substitute for being there
Boyle’s response was unusually detailed reporting:

- Describe the apparatus and procedure sufficiently for another investigator to attempt **replication**, performing the experiment again to check the result.
- Record when and where observations occurred.
- Identify people present as witnesses.
- Separate observed events from proposed explanations.

These details can have scientific relevance rather than being mere fussiness. Air-pressure observations may differ with weather or altitude. A stormy day and a sunny day, or a mountain and a low-lying location, need not produce the same readings. Recording conditions permits later investigators to assess which differences matter instead of having to assume they do not.

The lecturer contrasts Boyle’s extensive descriptions with modern articles that may depend on readers’ specialist knowledge or further contact with authors for procedural detail. The point is a contrast in reporting practices, not an assignment rule or proof that every modern article is impossible to replicate.

The tension is central: detailed written reports still involve words and some reliance on other people. They make checking more possible rather than abolishing testimony. Even a strong commitment to direct observation had to be implemented through social practices—meetings, witnesses, reports, and cooperation.

## Hobbes and Boyle: from effects to causes, or causes to effects?

Careful data collection does not eliminate the need for reasoning. If the data concern particular observations but the claim concerns a general cause, a **warrant** must explain why those observations support that causal claim, and **backing** must support the warrant. Simply increasing the number of observations does not supply every missing inferential link.

The lecture discusses Boyle’s account of the **spring of the air**, the period’s language for air’s spring-like or elastic behavior. The exact alternative mechanisms proposed by Hobbes are not supplied, so the teaching concerns their argumentative role rather than a detailed comparison of their physics.

## Generalizing effects is different from explaining them
Repeated trials may support a generalization such as: with this kind of apparatus and procedure, this kind of result tends to occur. That is not yet an identification of the underlying mechanical cause.

**Abduction**, also called **inference to the best explanation**, proposes a hypothesis because it explains the evidence better than relevant competitors. Unlike a deductively guaranteed conclusion, it depends on a comparison of possible explanations and on reasons for preferring one. Showing that a hypothesis can explain an observation is not enough if other hypotheses can explain it too.

Hobbes challenged Boyle by producing alternative causal stories consistent with the observations. Their availability undermines a move directly from “my mechanism explains these results” to “these results establish my mechanism.” It does not automatically show that Boyle’s mechanism is false, nor does mere possibility establish that every alternative is equally good. To defend a best-explanation inference, Boyle would need grounds for preferring his account or evidence that discriminates among the alternatives.

## Opposite directions of inquiry
Boyle’s Baconian approach works cautiously from observed **effects** toward their causes. Hobbes argued that science should instead proceed from rationally established correct knowledge of **causes** to knowledge of the effects they produce. For Hobbes, understanding the causes should guide the interpretation of what is observed, rather than being built solely from an accumulation of observed outcomes.

Both participated seriously in the intellectual world associated with the Scientific Revolution, yet they disagreed over the direction and standards of scientific reasoning. Their disagreement is therefore evidence against a historical narrative in which everyone adopted the same observation-first method.

## Newton’s prisms and the ideal of a crucial experiment

## The two-prism example
It was already known that passing light through a prism could produce a spectrum, a spread of colors. The lecture also discusses recombining separated colors into one beam. The central result is more specific: Newton selected red light from the first prism’s output and refracted it again through a second prism. **Refraction** is the change in a light beam’s direction as it passes through a medium such as a prism. The selected red beam remained red rather than becoming another rainbow.

This matters because it tests whether passing light through a prism produces a new range of colors from whatever beam enters, or instead reveals differences already associated with the incoming light. In the lecture’s simplified contrast, a further rainbow and a still-red beam are the rival expected outcomes. The result favors the account under which the isolated color remains that color through further refraction.

**Diagram caveat:** the lecturer questioned the middle row of the displayed prism diagram and told students to focus on its top and bottom rows. Do not treat the middle row as an additional verified result. The supported result for this discussion is the isolated red beam remaining red on refraction through another prism.

## What makes an experiment “crucial”?
A **crucial experiment** is intended to discriminate decisively between competing theories, rather than merely add one more observation. Newton treated the experiment’s discriminating force—its evidential weight—as more important than accumulating a large number of similar trials. His reports did not reproduce Boyle’s detailed lists of dates, witnesses, and circumstances, because he did not regard those particulars as necessary to the argument.

The underlying reasoning can be reconstructed conditionally:

1. The relevant alternatives predict different outcomes under the specified setup.
2. The experiment is correctly performed and the outcome reliably observed.
3. The observed outcome conflicts with one alternative’s prediction and fits the other’s.
4. Therefore, the result counts against the first alternative and in favor of the second.

To move from that discrimination to certainty that the remaining theory is true, further conditions matter: the rivals must exhaust the relevant possibilities, their predictions must actually follow under the experimental conditions, and errors or other explanations must not account for the outcome. Otherwise, eliminating one proposal need not establish the only correct account of nature. This makes explicit what a decisive-elimination argument requires; it is not a claim that the lecture independently checked all these conditions for Newton.

## Newton versus Boyle—and Shapin’s conclusion
Newton sought theoretical certainty comparable to mathematical proof, using mathematical as well as experimental tools. Boyle cautiously separated observations from speculative explanations and supplied extensive particulars to support checking and repetition. Hobbes, meanwhile, wanted knowledge of causes to precede knowledge of effects.

These differences concern more than personalities. They concern what evidence deserves emphasis, how experimental results connect to theory, how much repetition is needed, and how confident a conclusion may be. Newton’s claim of certainty is his methodological position as presented in the lecture, not an instruction that students must regard a single experiment as infallible.

Together, the examples explain Shapin’s main argument: even prominent Scientific Revolution figures did not share one uniform recipe for producing knowledge. The historical account is therefore more complex than everyone abandoning books and simply looking at the world. This is a supported, revisable interpretation, not the final lecture’s joking suggestion of a proof valid for eternity.
