# HPS100: Induction and deduction — review and guidance

Unofficial student study notes. Consult official course materials for current requirements. Practice questions are not official assessments.

## Revision checklist and traps

- Identify premises, conclusion, and the inference connecting them. Indicator words help, but arguments can be implicit; causal “because” may explain an accepted fact.
- Test validity by asking whether all premises could be true while the conclusion is false. Do not confuse validity with true premises.
- Recognize the valid dog/Sam forms and both invalid reversals. A cute non-dog defeats the two fallacies.
- Induction extends observations to unobserved cases without a deductive guarantee. Informative exceptions specify conditions, not “except when false.”
- Distinguish Toulmin data, claim, warrant, backing, qualifier, and reservation. Confidence is not scope; warrants and backing are often unstated.
- Reconstruct Hume's two branches: nonuniformity is conceivable; using past success to justify future reliability is circular. His challenge concerns justification, not merely certainty.
- Separate explaining why people believe from justifying what they believe. Practical necessity is not automatically epistemic support.
- Explanatory inference needs comparison with alternatives. “Probably” alone does not fix affirming the consequent.
- The explanatory response challenges Hume's two-option assumption, but must defend uniformity as the best explanation.
- Target the exact disputed part of an argument while preserving agreement elsewhere.

## Instructor guidance for analysis and study

Use Toulmin-style analysis for the assigned argument work. Keep each entry short, direct, and understandable: a few sentences rather than a page-long discussion. Aim to see all the parts together, not produce an analysis much longer than its passage.

Use context from the surrounding reading to reconstruct implicit warrants and backing. Do not force an exception into every argument: write **none** when no reservation is appropriate. An author describing someone else's argument is acceptable source material.

Do the practice yourself; pencil-and-paper practice is recommended because the midterm and exam require writing that way. For multiple choice, prioritize central concepts discussed extensively in lecture rather than memorizing names, dates, or every sentence of the readings. Technical Latin names and advanced formal proofs are not memorization requirements here.

For further philosophy research, the instructor recommended the peer-reviewed Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. PHL245 was suggested for students wanting deeper formal logic, not as a course prerequisite.
