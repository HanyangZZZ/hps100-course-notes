# HPS100: Induction and deduction

Unofficial student study notes. Consult official course materials for current requirements. Practice questions are not official assessments.

## 1. Arguments: reasons, not fights

An **argument** is an idea or set of ideas offered as a reason to believe another idea. Its **premises** supply the reasons; its **conclusion** is what those reasons support. An **inference** is the move that uses one idea to justify another. The argument includes the premises, conclusion, and supporting connection—not merely its final sentence.

Examples identify these roles without yet establishing that the reasoning is good:
- **My legs are itchy → what I stepped in was poison ivy.** The itchiness is the premise; poison ivy is the proposed conclusion. Other causes of itching remain possible.
- **The lights are on → someone is home.** The visible lights supply a reason for the conclusion, not a guarantee.
- **I am hungry → we should make dinner.** This remains an argument whether phrased using “therefore,” “since,” or “implies,” or simply as “I'm hungry. Let's make dinner.” Context can communicate the inference without an explicit indicator.

Useful indicators include **therefore, since, thus, implies, consequently, it follows that,** and **given that**. They are clues, not mechanical tests. In particular, **because** can introduce either a reason for believing something or an explanation of an already accepted fact. If everyone agrees that someone's hair is wet, saying that it is wet because it rained explains the wetness; it is not necessarily trying to establish a doubtful conclusion.

Argument exchange can be cooperative knowledge-making: participants use shared starting points to reach an understanding neither brought to the conversation alone. A controversial premise may need its own supporting argument before it can persuade someone. This matters throughout science, philosophy, and history, which use reasons rather than simply collecting assertions.

## 2. Deduction, validity, and counterexamples

A **deductively valid** argument guarantees that its conclusion is true **if its premises are true**. Validity concerns this logical relationship, not whether we already know the premises to be true. An attempted deductive argument can fail this test and be invalid.

Take: all dogs are cute; Sam is a dog; therefore Sam is cute. There is no possible case in which both premises are true and the conclusion false. Doubts about whether all dogs really are cute, or whether Sam actually is a dog, do not undermine its validity. They concern the premises instead.

The lecture used these forms, with the terms held fixed:

| Form | Example and result |
|---|---|
| All X are Y; A is X; therefore A is Y | All dogs are cute; Sam is a dog; therefore Sam is cute. Valid: the lecture's modus ponens example. |
| All X are Y; A is not Y; therefore A is not X | All dogs are cute; Sam is not cute; therefore Sam is not a dog. Valid: modus tollens. |
| All X are Y; A is Y; therefore A is X | All dogs are cute; Sam is cute; therefore Sam is a dog. Invalid: affirming the consequent. Sam could be a cute human. |
| All X are Y; A is not X; therefore A is not Y | All dogs are cute; Sam is not a dog; therefore Sam is not cute. Invalid: denying the antecedent. Sam could again be a cute human. |

A **counterexample to validity** preserves every premise while making the conclusion false. It is not enough merely to reject a premise. “All dogs are cute” does not say that **only** dogs are cute; overlooking that difference produces both fallacies above.

Deduction makes explicit what the premises already entail. This is useful, but does not by itself explain science's move beyond observed cases to new claims about nature. The displayed advanced formal proof illustrated a field of study, not a technique students were expected to learn here.

**Reading clarification — Godfrey, PDF p. 4:** A valid argument can have false premises and a true or false conclusion. Validity alone therefore does not certify the actual truth of the conclusion; truth of the premises must also be secured.

## 3. Induction: generalizing beyond observations

In the lecture's central use, **induction** moves from particular observations to a generalization. A successful inductive argument supports its conclusion without guaranteeing it. Its purpose is to make the conclusion more credible, not logically unavoidable.

The classic example is: **1,000 observed swans were white; therefore all swans are white.** The premises could be true even though an unobserved black swan exists. A sample confined to places with white swans can hide that exception. Merely increasing the count does not turn this into a deductive guarantee. The expression **black swan event** was connected to this possibility of an unexpected case defeating an established expectation.

Requiring observation of every case would severely restrict science. Consider **copper is conductive**: must we inspect all copper in the universe before making the generalization? Science needs claims extending beyond the inspected sample. Repeated successful tests can increase confidence and help make hypotheses more specific, even without proof understood as certainty. The lecture's percentages expressed informal confidence, not calculated probabilities.

Scientific laws also differ in their apparent scope. Biological generalizations such as **cats have four legs** admit developmental exceptions and variations in limb or toe number. The **Pauli exclusion principle**, presented as forbidding two fermions from occupying the same quantum state simultaneously, illustrated an apparently exceptionless physical principle. The lecturer connected exclusion to the everyday fact that a hand does not pass through a table, as a qualitative illustration rather than a complete account of matter's resistance to interpenetration. The point was not a quantum-mechanics derivation: even very strong confidence is different from having inspected every instance.

Saying **all swans are white except when they are not** avoids being wrong but supplies no informative conditions. Likewise, adding “unless they can” to the fermion example does not explain an exception. Useful limitations identify where or when a claim applies.

**Logical correction to the classroom exchange:** “It is not the case that all swans are not white” means **at least one swan is white**, not **all swans are white**. One white swan establishes the former but not the latter. The suggested equivalence in the spoken discussion should not be learned as a logical rule. The ravens paradox was explicitly deferred rather than taught.

**Reading clarification — Godfrey, PDF pp. 4–5:** No finite collection of observations conclusively establishes an unrestricted universal generalization with infinitely many possible applications; one appropriate counterinstance can refute it. Godfrey-Smith uses “induction” narrowly for generalizing from particulars, while some authors use it broadly for good nondeductive reasoning. This terminology matters when explanatory inference is introduced later.

## 4. Observations, theories, and background frameworks

Observations are essential to science, but science also seeks **generalizations, models, theories, and laws** that explain and predict. A central philosophical question is what licenses the move from observation statements to these broader claims.

The lecture's deliberately simplified example was **metals expand when heated**. Observations that this metal expanded and that another metal expanded provide a base for generalization. They do not automatically rule out every untested exception. Treat the example as a model of an inference, not a complete physical law with all conditions specified. Identifying an important difference between a toy example and actual scientific practice can itself be useful criticism.

**Conservation of energy** complicated the picture. The lecture contrasted mechanists, who understood life through chemical processes, with vitalists, who proposed an additional vital force. In its frog-leg illustration, electrical stimulation produced movement, and chemical changes were compared with the energy available from corresponding changes outside living tissue. The intended lesson was that ordinary chemical processes could account for the energetic change without a special vital essence.

Even a successful experiment on such a system would not prove energy conservation everywhere and always.

Conservation can subsequently function as a **background framework** through which observations are interpreted, rather than simply as a claim rebuilt from a list of confirming observations. An apparent energy imbalance in an ordinary chemistry experiment would usually first prompt checking the experiment, not immediate abandonment of conservation. Thus evidence and theory interact: established commitments influence how surprising results are assessed.

**Reading clarification — Godfrey, PDF p. 2:** The classical problem of induction asks why observed patterns justify claims about unobserved cases. The broader problem of **confirmation** asks how an observation can be evidence for a theory at all. Not every scientific support relation is just a collection of instances followed by a generalization.

## 5. Toulmin analysis: make the supporting structure explicit

The course uses a six-part **Toulmin-style analysis**, especially for informal inductive arguments. It separates the evidence from the rule used to interpret it, and that rule from its justification.

| Part | Question to ask | Heated-metals example |
|---|---|---|
| **Data** | What premises or evidence are offered? | Ten different metals were observed being heated on 100 different occasions, and all expanded. |
| **Claim** | What conclusion is supported? | Metals expand when heated. |
| **Warrant** | What rule connects these data to this claim? | Repeated observations across different contexts permit a generalization about how things behave. |
| **Backing** | Why accept that warrant? | The future will resemble the past: nature behaves uniformly. |
| **Qualifier** | How confidently is the claim asserted? | We are mostly confident, rather than certain. |
| **Reservation** | Under what circumstances would the claim not apply? | Possible exceptions under extreme gravity or electromagnetic conditions. |

The example's backing is a proposal to examine, not an already established philosophical foundation. Hume will challenge exactly this step.

**Confidence and scope are different.** “Most metals expand” can be asserted with very high confidence; “all metals expand” can be asserted with only moderate confidence. A qualifier reports the strength of commitment. A reservation identifies excluded cases or conditions. The lecture's black-hole scenario made this distinction vivid: heating and expansion under ordinary conditions need not describe metal undergoing extreme compression. It was an illustrative possible reservation, not a worked astrophysical result. A reservation should do more than say “except when false.”

Warrants and especially backing are often **implicit**. An author may give the observations and conclusion without spelling out why generalization is permissible. Analysis reconstructs those connections using the passage and its wider context. Qualifiers and reservations may also require interpretation. Do not confuse a defensible reconstruction with something the author explicitly said; sometimes backing remains unclear. If no reservation is warranted, **none** is acceptable.

Different disciplines encourage different scopes: historians often make time-and-place-specific claims; physics often aims at universal laws; biological and psychological claims may require tighter limits. A psychological study of particular undergraduates does not automatically establish a universal law of thought. Breadth also does not equal ease: modelling an electron differs from modelling an open ecosystem with many species, climate, geology, and no convenient boundary. “Hard science” should not be taken as a simple ranking of difficulty.

## 6. Hume's problem: what justifies uniformity?

The proposed backing for ordinary induction is **uniformity of nature**: unexperienced instances will resemble experienced ones, and nature's course will remain uniform. A familiar expectation illustrates the assumption: when a cue ball hits the other billiard balls, we expect them to move normally, not become soap bubbles or float away.

Hume asks what justifies this expectation. The lecture reconstructed two possible routes:

1. **Deductive justification:** Is uniformity logically necessary? Hume argues that a change in nature is conceivable without contradiction. If nonuniformity is possible, uniformity cannot be established as a necessary logical truth. Contrast ordinary arithmetic: with the meanings fixed, 1 + 1 = 3 contradicts the relevant concepts.
2. **Inductive justification:** Can successful past experience establish uniformity? Saying that nature has behaved regularly before and therefore will continue doing so already assumes that the past licenses conclusions about the future. This uses the disputed principle to establish itself: it is circular.

Russell's **chicken** illustrates the danger of extrapolation. Each day the farmer feeds and protects it, until the day the farmer kills it. The regularity on which its expectation rested ends. This is not a literal example of the laws of nature changing; the lecture distinguished it from Hume's conceptual argument.

Several tempting replies leave the problem unresolved. Citing laws discovered from past observations inherits the same difficulty. Saying uniformity has never been disproved again relies on the past. Saying that unchanged circumstances will produce the same results assumes what is being questioned. Treating uniformity as an axiom may describe a practical commitment, but is not the missing independent argument.

Crucially, Hume's challenge is **stronger than “induction is not certain.”** It questions whether the inference has rational justification at all. If the backing cannot be justified, the generalizing warrant loses its proposed foundation. This is not evidence that nature actually will change.

**Reading clarification — Hume, PDF pp. 1–4:** Repeated experience of flame and heat gives a pattern of conjunction. In a new case, only one item may be perceived, and the mind supplies its usual companion. Repetition does not by itself disclose a necessary connection. Hume's distinction between demonstrative and probable reasoning explains why neither route supplies a noncircular proof of resemblance between observed and unobserved instances.

## 7. Reconstructing and evaluating Hume's multi-step argument

The lecture's overall Toulmin reconstruction was:
- **Claim:** Induction is unjustified.
- **Data:** It cannot be justified inductively or deductively.
- **Warrant:** If neither route can justify it, it cannot be justified.
- **Backing:** Induction and deduction exhaust the available kinds of inference.
- **Qualifier:** Highly confident; the lecturer allowed interpretive room for another justification to be found.
- **Reservation:** None identified for the overall claim.

Its data need arguments of their own. **P1:** no inductive justification. **P2:** no deductive justification. Together they establish **P3:** neither route works. P3 supports the final conclusion only with the additional exhaustiveness assumption. Eliminating two possibilities is a deductive move; concluding that no possibilities remain requires that they were the only possibilities.

For the **inductive branch**, the data are that induction requires uniformity and cannot establish it without already relying on it. The warrant rejects circular reasoning; the backing is that assuming the disputed conclusion does not supply an independent reason to accept it. The slide's proposed qualifier distinguished justification from being acceptable in practice. The lecturer corrected its placement: this is better treated as a **reservation about what the criticism covers**, not as a degree of confidence. It excludes a moral condemnation or instruction to stop using induction.

For the **deductive branch**, the claim is that induction cannot be justified deductively. The data are the conceivability of nonuniform nature; the warrant moves from conceivability to possibility; the slide supplies the backing that imagination accesses the structure of concepts. The slide presents this branch as certain, with no reservation. Those are reconstructed positions, not the lecturer's endorsement of every premise.

A focused objection challenges the move from **what we can imagine** to **what is possible**. Imagination may reveal a psychological fact rather than the structure of reality. One can agree with the conclusion that uniformity is not deductively demonstrable while rejecting this particular supporting argument.

Hume's practical response is that people will continue making inductions naturally. An evolutionary story—ancestors who anticipated patterns survived more successfully—might explain that tendency, but explaining a belief's origin does not establish its rational justification. The lecturer also illustrated this distinction with fear of spiders or snakes despite no personal encounter with a deadly one: an explanation of how a fear arose does not by itself show that a present threat warrants it. Similarly, needing induction for everyday life does not itself show that we can trust it.

**Reading clarification — Hume, PDF pp. 6–8:** Hume explains the mental transition through association and habit: a familiar impression calls up its usual companion, much as a familiar word calls up an idea. This psychological explanation is not a noncircular rational proof of uniformity.

Argument analysis makes criticism cooperative and precise. Agree on the parts you accept, then isolate the disputed warrant, backing, or premise. Maximizing disagreement obscures the issue; locating the smallest important disagreement makes progress possible.

## 8. Explanatory inference and its limits

**Explanatory inference**, also called **abduction** or **inference to the best explanation**, offers a possible challenge to the assumption that deduction and enumerative induction are the only options. Instead of extending a sampled regularity, it proposes a hypothesis that explains observed evidence.

The tempting short form is: **if H, then O; O; therefore probably H**. Without further support, this resembles affirming the consequent. Adding “probably” does not automatically repair it. A defensible inference needs a comparative premise: H is the **best available explanation**, with no known alternative that is equally good or better.

Three examples show the difference:
- **Cold:** A cold would cause coughing and sneezing; those symptoms occur; therefore perhaps a cold explains them. Known severe allergies during ragweed season can undermine the inference by providing a better alternative.
- **Stolen money:** Secret theft would leave someone broke; they are broke; therefore someone is stealing. This is weak if having no job and spending their savings already explains the situation.
- **General relativity:** The theory predicts deflection of light near the Sun. During the 1919 eclipse, observations supported the predicted deflection. The eclipse made observations near the Sun feasible, and precise predicted direction and magnitude mattered. This supports a theory explaining the observations rather than generalizing from a thousand identical cases. It does not establish certainty: a competing theory might account for the same evidence. The lecture presented the example as having no equally good available rival; it did not establish that no possible rival could exist.

In contrast, **if H implies O, and not-O, then not-H** is deductively valid. The lecture used absent light deflection as a hypothetical falsification of relativity. **Reading clarification — Godfrey, PDF p. 8:** Actual scientific predictions usually require auxiliary assumptions, so a failed prediction may challenge the combined theory-and-assumptions package rather than uniquely identify which part failed.

The assignment example supplied another explanatory case: unusually high **iridium and related rare-element concentrations** occur in geological layers described in the passage as about 65 million years old; these elements occur at higher concentrations in meteors than near Earth's surface; a meteor impact explains the layer's composition. This supports the Alvarez impact hypothesis. **Reading clarification — Godfrey, PDF pp. 5–6:** This is not a generalization from many observed ancient impacts; it infers an explanatory past process. The paragraph was shown as suitable assignment material, not fully analyzed in class.

Applied to Hume, the proposal is: nature exhibits regularities; uniformity best explains them; therefore nature is probably uniform. This targets the **exhaustiveness backing** in his overall argument rather than simply counting more past regularities.

The lecture introduced **Al-Ghazali's alternative**: regular events depend on God's repeated free decisions, not laws that constrain an omnipotent God. God could decide differently, making miracles possible. Thus observed regularity can be given an alternative explanation that does not guarantee continuation. Whether the explanatory response succeeds depends on comparing such alternatives, not merely naming uniformity as a hypothesis.

A persistent limitation is that nobody has an assured inventory of every possible explanation. “Best among those considered” is weaker than “best possible.” Explanatory quality and rival hypotheses must be assessed, and no numerical probability rule was established here. The lecture offered this as a response to Hume worth evaluating, not a settled solution.
