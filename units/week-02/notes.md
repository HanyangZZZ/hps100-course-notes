## Arguments: reasons, conclusions, and cooperative inquiry

## What an argument is
In this course, an **argument** is an idea or set of ideas offered as a reason to believe another idea—not a fight. Its **premises** are the reasons offered; its **conclusion** is the claim those reasons are meant to support. An **inference** is the reasoning connection: using one idea to justify another. The argument includes the premises, the conclusion, and this supporting relationship.

Science, philosophy, and history use arguments to move from what is already accepted toward something further. Unit 1 examines how scientific knowledge is produced: this week approaches the question philosophically, by examining arguments and their justification; next week approaches it historically through the Scientific Revolution. Exchanging arguments can be cooperative: participants pool reasons and discover implications that neither had previously recognized. Premises therefore need some shared acceptance, or further arguments supporting them. Simply asserting a controversial premise will not persuade someone who rejects it.

## Recognizing the parts
- **Itchy legs → poison ivy:** “My legs are itchy” is the premise; the conclusion is that what I stepped in was poison ivy. The symptom is offered as evidence for a cause. This identifies the reasoning, not a guarantee that the diagnosis is correct.
- **Lights on → someone home:** the lights being on are the premise; someone being home is the conclusion. Again, naming an inference does not establish its strength.
- **Hungry → make dinner:** being hungry is offered as a reason for the practical conclusion that we should make dinner. The wording can change without changing the underlying argument: “Since I'm hungry, we should make dinner” and “I'm hungry; let's make dinner” can perform the same reasoning.

Words such as **therefore, since, thus, implies, consequently, it follows that**, and **given that** often signal an inference. They are clues, not necessary ingredients: context can supply the connection without an indicator word.

## Argument versus causal explanation
**Because** can introduce a reason for believing a claim, but it can also explain an already accepted event. If everyone agrees that my hair is wet and I say it is wet because it rained, I am explaining its cause, not trying to establish that it is wet. Ask what the speaker is doing: supporting a disputed claim, or explaining why an accepted fact occurred? The word alone cannot decide.

## Using analysis to disagree productively
Breaking an argument into parts lets you preserve agreement and locate the exact dispute. You might accept someone's conclusion and evidence while rejecting the rule linking them. That is more productive than objecting to every part at once: it makes clear which issue needs work, rather than turning inquiry into a fight.

## Deduction: validity, forms, and counterexamples

## A guarantee conditional on the premises
A **deductively valid argument** is one in which the premises cannot all be true while the conclusion is false. Its guarantee is conditional: **if** the premises are true, the conclusion must be true. Validity does not itself establish that the premises are actually true.

Example:
1. All dogs are cute.
2. Sam is a dog.
3. Therefore, Sam is cute.

The first premise includes every dog in the cute category; the second places Sam in that category of dogs. Sam therefore cannot be excluded from the cute category while both premises remain true. Whether some real dogs are not cute, or whether Sam is actually a person, concerns the truth of the premises—not the validity of this inference.

**Reading clarification — Godfrey, PDF p. 4:** A valid argument can have false premises, and in that case its conclusion can be either true or false. Validity alone does not certify an actual conclusion.

## Two valid patterns
An **argument form** is the underlying arrangement of premises and conclusion, independent of the particular subject matter. Keep the meanings of its terms fixed when substituting examples.

- **Modus ponens:** All Xs are Ys; A is an X; therefore A is a Y. The lecture's example is the dogs-and-Sam argument. In conditional wording: if Sam is a dog, Sam is cute; Sam is a dog; therefore Sam is cute.
- **Modus tollens:** All Xs are Ys; A is not a Y; therefore A is not an X. Example: all dogs are cute; Sam is not cute; therefore Sam is not a dog. If Sam were a dog, the first premise would require Sam to be cute, contradicting the second premise. Being a dog is therefore ruled out.

The instructor does not require memorizing these Latin names. Understanding the reasoning matters.

## Two invalid patterns
A **fallacy** here is an invalid inference pattern. A **counterexample to validity** is a possible case in which all premises are true but the conclusion is false.

- **Affirming the consequent:** All dogs are cute; Sam is cute; therefore Sam is a dog. Sam could be a cute person. Both premises would then be true and the conclusion false. The first premise says dogs are cute, not that only dogs are cute.
- **Denying the antecedent:** All dogs are cute; Sam is not a dog; therefore Sam is not cute. A cute human Sam again supplies a counterexample. The first premise places no restriction on how cute nondogs can be.

A false conclusion in one real example is not the definition of invalidity, and a true conclusion does not rescue an invalid pattern. The question is whether the premises guarantee it.

## Why the lecture moves beyond deduction
Deduction draws out what is already implied by its premises. It can reveal consequences we had not noticed, but it does not by itself justify extending a limited set of observations to every unobserved case. Science needs this further reach. That does not make deduction useless in science; it explains why the lecture concentrates on nondeductive support. The formal proof displayed in class illustrated the wider field of logic, not a proof students were expected to reproduce.

## Induction, generalizations, and the limits of observation

## From observed cases to a wider claim
**Induction**, in the narrower sense used here, moves from particular observations to a **generalization**: a claim that extends beyond those particular cases. A successful inductive argument makes its conclusion more credible without guaranteeing it. Merely having an inductive form does not ensure that an argument provides good support.

The classic example is: I have observed 1,000 swans and every one was white; therefore all swans are white. The conclusion includes swans never observed. A black swan is compatible with the premises, so those premises do not deductively guarantee the conclusion. The lecture uses geographically limited experience of white swans and the discovery of black swans to show why repeated observations can miss exceptions.

“All swans are white except when they are not” avoids being refuted but tells us nothing about which swans will be white. The equally empty “all swans are black except when they are not” makes the problem clear. A useful exception clause must specify conditions, not simply excuse every failure.

## Proof versus evidential confidence
If **proof** means establishing a claim with certainty, observations of some cases do not prove an unrestricted claim about all cases. But lack of proof does not make testing pointless. Repeated successful tests across different settings can increase confidence and help make a hypothesis more specific or exact. The lecture's percentage expressions illustrate confidence; they are not calculated probabilities from the evidence.

The question “Must we observe every swan?” becomes especially pressing for scientific claims such as copper being conductive: requiring inspection of all copper in the universe would prevent ordinary scientific generalization. Even very dependable-looking principles leave this philosophical question. The lecture invokes the **Pauli exclusion principle**, stated as no two fermions occupying the same quantum state at the same time, as a contrast to exception-prone biological claims. Here, fermions are a class of particles, and a quantum state describes a particle's physical condition in quantum theory. The lecturer also uses exclusion to illustrate why a hand does not simply pass through a table: exclusion constrains how the particles making up matter can occupy states together. This is a qualitative illustration, not a full explanation of the behavior of solid matter. Its role in the discussion is that confidence in a principle does not mean every possible instance has been checked.

**Reading clarification — Godfrey, PDF p. 4:** No finite collection of observations conclusively establishes an unrestricted generalization with infinitely many possible applications. One genuine counterinstance can refute that universal claim. This asymmetry concerns strict universals, not every statistical or qualified scientific claim.

## Why observations need interpretation
Observations are essential, but science seeks more than a list of separate events. **Theories, models, and laws** organize evidence into broader accounts that explain and predict. A central philosophical question is how observations support those accounts.

The toy law **all metals expand when heated** illustrates the transition: this metal expanded when heated, that one did too, and so on; therefore metals generally expand when heated. No finite run of successful instances alone rules out an unobserved exception—the lecture's “black swan” possibility. The toy law simplifies real scientific laws so the reasoning is visible; it should not be treated as a fully specified physical law.

Biological generalizations illustrate why scope matters: saying cats are four-legged does not mean every individual has exactly four legs. The lecture contrasts such exceptions with apparently broader physical regularities. These disciplinary contrasts are tendencies, not a rule that biology has no general laws or physics needs no qualifications.

## A negation trap
A brief classroom exchange treated “not all swans are nonwhite” as if it meant “all swans are white.” These are different. **Not all are nonwhite** says that at least one is white; **all are white** says that none is nonwhite. One white swan establishes the first, not the second. Keep the scope of “not” and “all” clear rather than carrying that tentative equivalence into your notes.

**Reading clarification — Godfrey, PDF pp. 2, 5–6:** **Confirmation** is the broader question of how observations support theories. Some authors use “induction” for all good nondeductive reasoning, but Godfrey-Smith distinguishes generalization from explanatory inference. The lecture follows this narrower distinction when introducing a third type of inference. Its induction problem is therefore related to, but not identical with, the whole problem of confirmation.

## Scientific principles can also organize observation

The conservation-of-energy discussion complicates a simple picture in which every scientific law is built by counting confirming instances.

The lecture contrasts **mechanism**, which explains living processes through ordinary physical and chemical processes, with **vitalism**, which invokes a special life force or vital essence. In its frog-leg illustration, investigators compare chemical changes and the energy associated with electrically stimulated movement in dead frog legs. The reported result is that the energy accounting fits what ordinary chemical change could supply, rather than requiring an additional vital source.

The point is the limited reach of such a result: an energy balance in one experimental system does not establish that energy is conserved everywhere and always. Nor does this brief illustration settle every historical version of vitalism. The account was an informal historical sketch, not a verified identification of a single decisive experiment.

Once conservation of energy becomes an established **background assumption**—a principle used in interpreting other experiments—it also shapes how scientists respond to observations. An apparent energy mismatch in an ordinary chemistry experiment usually prompts checking the apparatus, measurements, and calculations rather than immediately announcing the overthrow of conservation. Successful energy accounting reinforces the framework, while the framework helps determine what counts as an experimental error.

Thus the relationship runs both ways: evidence supports principles, and accepted principles organize evidence. The example is not a claim that a scientific principle can never be questioned.

## Toulmin analysis: the six parts and a complete worked example

The lecture adapts **Stephen Toulmin's** approach to analyzing arguments, particularly informal inductive arguments. It separates the evidence, the claim, and the assumptions that make the evidence relevant. It is not an automatic test that guarantees an argument is good.

## Six parts
1. **Data:** the premises or evidence offered. “Data” need not mean numerical measurements; a philosophical proposition can also function as a premise.
2. **Claim:** the conclusion being supported.
3. **Warrant:** the rule or inferential connection that licenses moving from these data to this claim. It answers, “Why would that evidence support this conclusion?”
4. **Backing:** the justification for accepting the warrant. It answers the next question: “Why trust that rule?”
5. **Qualifier:** the strength or degree of confidence attached to the claim, such as probably, very likely, or certain.
6. **Reservation:** circumstances excluded from the claim or conditions under which it would fail to apply.

## Worked example: heated metals
- **Data:** ten different metals were observed being heated on 100 different occasions, and all expanded.
- **Claim:** metals expand when heated.
- **Warrant:** observing a pattern repeatedly and in different contexts licenses generalizing it beyond the observed cases.
- **Backing:** nature is uniform—the relevant patterns will continue, so past observations can inform expectations about unobserved cases.
- **Qualifier:** high confidence rather than certainty.
- **Reservation:** ordinary conditions are intended; possible exceptions include extreme gravitational or electromagnetic conditions.

The data alone describe the tested cases. The warrant supplies permission to extend the pattern. The backing tries to explain why that permission is trustworthy. If the backing fails, this justification of the warrant is undermined even if the observations themselves are accurate. Hume's challenge will target exactly that support.

The black-hole scenario is a thought experiment about the claim's limits: material could be heated while extreme compression prevents the expected expansion. Its function is to motivate explicit scope conditions, not to provide a detailed model of matter near a singularity.

## Confidence is not scope
“I am extremely confident that **most** metals expand” and “I am moderately confident that **all** metals expand” differ in two ways. Most/all changes how widely the claim applies; extremely/moderately changes confidence in that claim. Do not put every limit into the qualifier. Reservations identify excluded circumstances, rather than saying only “except when it fails.”

## Reconstructing what is unstated
Ordinary speakers rarely state all six parts. A plumber may cite repeated experience with heated copper without saying aloud that repeated observations justify generalization, or explaining why nature should be uniform. Argument analysis makes such implicit commitments explicit.

Use the passage's context and the rest of the reading to infer what the author likely relies on. Distinguish an explicit statement from your reconstruction; do not invent arbitrary assumptions just to fill a box. Backing can be unclear, and different defensible analyses may divide the work differently. If there is genuinely no reservation, write **none**.

## Scope and subject matter
The instructor contrasts philosophers' and physicists' often-wide claims with historians' more time-and-place-specific claims. Claims in biology and psychology may need narrower populations or conditions. A result obtained from a particular group of undergraduates does not automatically establish a universal law of thought: the step from that sample to everyone needs support.

This is not a ranking of how intellectually difficult disciplines are. The lecture contrasts modeling one electron with modeling an open ecology involving many species, climate, geology, and no clean boundary. A narrower system may permit a different kind of explanation than a highly interacting system; the label “hard science” should not obscure that complexity.

## Practical method
Choose the conclusion first, identify the evidence actually offered, and then state the bridge between them. Ask why the author would accept that bridge, and finally separate confidence from exceptions. Keep each entry short and direct enough that you can see how the parts fit together. The instructor does not want a page-long discussion for every element or an analysis much longer than the original argument.

## Hume's problem of induction: why trust uniformity?

## The principle under examination
The **uniformity of nature** is the idea that unexperienced instances will resemble experienced ones in the relevant respects: the course of nature continues consistently. It does not say every event is identical. It says the patterns used to infer beyond our observations remain applicable.

For example, after repeatedly seeing a cue ball strike other billiard balls and make them move, we expect that response again—not an explosion into dust, soap bubbles, or the balls floating away. What justifies expecting the observed pattern to continue?

Hume's problem is not simply that induction lacks certainty. In the lecture's reconstruction, he challenges whether it has **rational justification at all**: whether we have a reason to trust conclusions about unobserved cases without already assuming that induction is reliable. A well-supported belief is different from one we merely cannot help holding.

## First route: deductive justification
Could uniformity be a logical truth, like 1 + 1 = 2? Hume argues that we can conceive of a change in nature without contradiction. He treats clear conceivability as evidence of possibility. If nature's changing is possible, then its remaining uniform is not logically necessary. Thus pure deduction does not establish the uniformity that induction needs.

The contrast with arithmetic matters: given the ordinary meanings of the terms, 1 + 1 = 3 is not an alternative course the world might take; it conflicts with the concepts. A change in natural regularities does not appear contradictory in that way.

Russell's chicken illustrates the risk of extending a pattern: the farmer feeds and protects it every day until killing it. The past pattern did not guarantee the next event. However, this is **not itself an example of the laws of nature changing**. It illustrates failed expectation under continuing laws, whereas Hume's logical argument concerns whether a change in nature is conceivable.

This route has a disputable premise. What someone can imagine is a psychological fact; it does not automatically establish what reality permits. You may agree that thinking alone does not establish uniformity while questioning this particular argument for that conclusion.

## Second route: justification from experience
Could we establish uniformity by observing that nature has been uniform so far? The proposed reasoning is: past regularities continued reliably; therefore they will continue in unobserved cases. But that is already the type of inference being questioned.

**Circular reasoning** uses the disputed claim or entitlement in the attempt to justify that very claim or entitlement. Past success can support future success only if experience legitimately extends to unobserved cases. Invoking past success to establish that legitimacy presupposes what it is meant to establish. Saying no failure has been observed, or appealing to laws themselves inferred from past observations, does not escape the same dependence.

**Reading clarification — Hume, PDF pp. 3–4:** Hume distinguishes **demonstrative reasoning**, which aims to show that denying a conclusion would involve a contradiction, from **probable reasoning**, which uses experience to support claims about unobserved cases. Here, “probable” does not mean a calculated percentage. Probable reasoning already relies on observed and unobserved cases resembling one another. Using it to justify that resemblance therefore assumes the very point being questioned: it is circular. Hume is challenging this justification, not showing that nature will actually change.

## Why these two failures would matter
The overall argument needs an **exhaustiveness assumption**: deduction and induction are the only possible routes to rational justification. “Exhaustive” means that the list includes every possibility. If another route exists, rejecting these two has not ruled it out. Only if the list is complete can Hume move from “neither of these routes works” to “no route works.” The lecture later introduces explanatory inference to challenge that assumption.

Applied to the metals argument, Hume's challenge leaves the observed expansions intact but attacks the backing for generalizing. If uniformity is indispensable backing and has no rational justification, the warrant has not been justified merely by amassing more observations. Reducing “certain” to “probably” does not by itself answer this deeper challenge.

## Explaining a belief is not justifying it
An evolutionary story could explain why creatures that extrapolate from experience survive better than those that do not. That explains why we have the tendency; it does not by itself supply a noncircular rational justification for trusting it. The lecture uses instinctive fear as a parallel: understanding why a response developed is different from showing that its present application is warranted.

Likewise, the fact that ordinary life requires induction shows its practical importance, not automatically its rational credentials. Treating uniformity as an axiom means accepting it as a starting point; that is not the missing proof.

As presented here, Hume does not demand that people stop using induction or regard themselves as morally at fault for doing so. It is a natural tendency that will continue. His skeptical conclusion concerns its justification, not a command to abandon daily life.

**Reading addition — Hume, PDF pp. 1–2, 6–8:** Hume develops a psychological account through **constant conjunction**, the repeated occurrence of similar things in the same relations of proximity and sequence. Repeated experiences of flame with heat lead the sight of flame to call up an expectation of heat. Repetition creates an association in thought; it does not reveal a further necessary connection that logically compels the effect. This clarifies why an account of our mental transition can coexist with skepticism about its rational justification.

## Reconstructing Hume as linked arguments

## Main argument
The lecture's Toulmin reconstruction is:
- **Claim:** induction is unjustified.
- **Data:** induction cannot be justified inductively or deductively.
- **Warrant:** if something cannot be justified by either of these methods, it cannot be justified.
- **Backing:** induction and deduction are the only possible methods of justification.
- **Qualifier:** highly likely, with interpretive room for another way of justifying it to be found.
- **Reservation:** none identified.

The qualifier is the instructor's interpretation, not an explicit declaration by Hume. The argument is being reconstructed rather than transcribed into boxes word for word.

The data are substantial philosophical claims, not self-evident observations. Someone who disputes them needs the supporting arguments. This is how a **multi-step argument** works: a conclusion established at one stage becomes a premise at the next.

The two supporting arguments aim to establish that induction cannot justify itself and that deduction cannot justify it either. Together, these conclusions rule out **those two routes**. They do not yet rule out every possible route.

To reach the stronger conclusion that induction cannot be justified at all, the main argument needs the **exhaustiveness assumption** explained above: there are no other routes. If we accept that assumption, the final step is a **deductive inference**—every possible option has been ruled out, so none remains.

Notice what still needs defending: the two supporting conclusions and the claim that the list of routes is complete. The conclusion follows if we accept those premises; describing the final step as deductive does not establish their truth. It is also consistent to use deduction to examine the limits of induction.

## Supporting argument 1: no inductive justification
- **Claim:** induction cannot be justified inductively.
- **Data:** induction relies on uniformity; an appeal to past experience to establish uniformity already uses that reliance.
- **Warrant:** a circular argument does not independently justify what it presupposes.
- **Backing:** a justification must provide a reason that does not simply assume the disputed entitlement.
- **Qualifier:** no distinct confidence qualifier was clearly established in the classroom discussion.
- **Reservation or scope clarification:** this concerns rational justification, not whether it is morally bad or practically unacceptable to use induction.

The slide's terse backing, “This is not how arguments work,” is made explicit here: the alleged support depends on the very permission to generalize that it is supposed to establish. The instructor initially placed the distinction between justification and being okay under qualifier, then corrected it to **reservation**. Retain the correction rather than copying the uncorrected slide classification.

## Supporting argument 2: no deductive justification
- **Claim:** induction cannot be justified deductively through establishing uniformity as necessary.
- **Data:** a world in which nature is not uniform can be imagined.
- **Warrant:** what can be clearly imagined is possible.
- **Backing:** imagination gives access to the structure of concepts, allowing us to recognize possibilities rather than contradictions.
- **Qualifier:** the slide presents the claim as certain within this reconstruction.
- **Reservation:** none listed on the slide.

The missing connecting step is important: if nonuniformity is possible, uniformity is not logically necessary; it therefore cannot serve as a demonstrated logical truth underwriting induction. The instructor questions the warrant/backing link because the limits of imagination need not match the limits of reality. The slide's confidence label does not make that criticism disappear.

## What analysis achieves
These reconstructions allow a targeted disagreement. You can accept the no-circularity argument while challenging conceivability as a guide to possibility. Or you can accept both supporting conclusions while challenging the main argument's claim that there are only two kinds of inference. Changing one part does not require rejecting every other part or the overall practical importance of induction.

## Explanatory inference: hypotheses, rival explanations, and tests

## A third kind of inference
**Explanatory inference**, also called **abduction** or **inference to the best explanation**, supports a hypothesis because it explains the observed evidence better than available rivals. It is neither a deductive guarantee nor simply an accumulation of similar instances followed by a generalization.

Its rough structure is:
1. If hypothesis H were true, observation O would be expected.
2. O is observed.
3. H is the best available explanation of O, with no equally good or better available rival.
4. Therefore, H is probably true.

The third step does essential work. Without it, the pattern resembles affirming the consequent: H could produce O, but something else could produce O too. Merely adding “probably” to an invalid deductive inference does not make the reasoning strong. The explanation must be good, and the alternatives must be assessed.

**Reading clarification — Godfrey, PDF pp. 5–6:** Scientific evidence often supports an explanatory process or structure rather than a universal generalization from repeated instances. Godfrey-Smith allows more than one kind of nondeductive inference rather than assuming all scientific support reduces to simple induction.

## Cold versus allergies
“If I have a cold, I would be coughing and sneezing; I am coughing and sneezing; therefore I probably have a cold.” Symptoms are compatible with a cold, but compatibility alone does not make it the best explanation. If I have severe allergies and it is ragweed season, allergies provide a relevant rival. That weakens the conclusion without deductively proving that I do not have a cold.

## Theft versus ordinary spending
“If someone secretly stole my money, I would be broke; I am broke; therefore someone probably stole my money.” Being unemployed and having spent one's savings can explain the same observation without theft. The example demonstrates why an observed consequence is not enough to identify its cause, even when the conclusion is qualified as probable.

## Relativity and the 1919 eclipse
The lecture presents the eclipse observation as a historical case of explanatory support. General relativity predicted that light passing near the Sun would be deflected, with a particular amount and direction. The eclipse made observation of stars near the Sun practicable despite its ordinary brightness. The reported observations agreed with the prediction, supporting the theory.

The important inference is not “we observed many instances, so every instance must be like that.” A small set of distinctive observations can support a theory because it explains them. The lecture emphasizes relativity's explanatory success and the absence of an equally good available alternative in the example. The relevant point is the discriminating success of the prediction, not a rule that mere light bending uniquely identifies a theory.

The support is still not deductive. An unconsidered theory might predict the same result. Further successful tests strengthen the case but do not turn “best available explanation” into “only logically possible explanation.”

## Confirmation versus deductive falsification
The simplified contrasting pattern is:
- If relativity is true, the light will bend.
- The light did not bend.
- Therefore relativity is not true.

This is modus tollens: the absence of a necessary consequence rules out the hypothesis, **provided the stated conditional and the observation are correct**. In contrast, observing the predicted bending does not deductively establish relativity, because another hypothesis might also predict it.

**Reading clarification — Godfrey, PDF p. 8:** Real hypotheses usually need **auxiliary assumptions**, additional assumptions about the setup and conditions, to entail an observation. A failed prediction then directly challenges the combination of theory and auxiliaries, rather than automatically identifying the theory alone as false. This qualifies the lecture's deliberately simple falsification example.

## Can explanatory inference answer Hume?

## Targeting the exhaustive-options assumption
Hume's reconstructed main argument says induction cannot be justified deductively or inductively, and assumes these are the only options. Explanatory inference challenges that **backing**. If it is a distinct legitimate form of support, ruling out the first two routes no longer automatically rules out every route.

But showing that there is another possible route is not the same as showing that it succeeds. A proposed explanatory defense is:
1. We observe extensive regularities in nature.
2. Nature's being uniform is the best explanation of those regularities.
3. Therefore, nature is probably uniform.

This differs from **enumerative induction**, which generalizes from repeated observed cases. The proposed argument instead asks why those regularities exist: it treats uniformity as a hypothesis that would explain them. If that explanatory inference is legitimate and uniformity is genuinely the best explanation, it could supply backing for induction.

## A rival: continuing divine choice
The lecture presents Al-Ghazali's alternative in the context of reconciling philosophy with an **omnipotent** (all-powerful) God. On this account, apparent natural regularities persist because God freely sustains the same outcomes on each occasion, not because God is bound by independent laws. God could choose otherwise, as in a miracle. The lecture's burning bush and staff becoming a snake illustrate the proposed possibility of exceptional divine action.

Both accounts can fit past regularity: nature is uniformly ordered, or divine choices have so far maintained the same pattern. They differ in what is supposed to explain the pattern and whether its continuation is secured. Merely noticing the regularity does not by itself choose between them. Whether one explanation is better requires an argument; the example is not offered as a proof of either theology or natural uniformity.

## The unfinished difficulty
We do not possess a verified list of every possible explanation. A hypothesis may be the best of those considered while an unknown rival would explain the evidence as well or better. That is a central reason explanatory inference remains fallible.

Consequently, the lecture presents an explanatory response as a possible answer to Hume, not a settled proof. It challenges the two-route assumption, but inherits questions about explanatory quality, alternatives, and the justification of explanatory inference itself. Our practical need for induction may make a reassuring solution attractive; that need alone does not establish its truth.
